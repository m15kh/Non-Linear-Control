Many equations contain a small parameter \(\varepsilon\): a weak nonlinearity, a light coupling, a small forcing. Perturbation theory turns this into an advantage: solve the simple problem at \(\varepsilon = 0\), then correct it in powers of \(\varepsilon\).

10.1 Approximate solutions

For \(\dot x = f(t, x, \varepsilon)\), expand the solution as a power series

\[ x(t, \varepsilon) = x_0(t) + \varepsilon x_1(t) + \cdots + \varepsilon^{N-1} x_{N-1}(t) + O(\varepsilon^N) \]

Substituting and matching powers of \(\varepsilon\) gives \(x_0\) from the nominal (often nonlinear) equation and each correction \(x_k\) from a linear equation driven by the earlier terms. On a finite time interval the error really is \(O(\varepsilon^N)\).

10.2 Perturbation on the infinite interval

On an infinite interval the regular expansion can fail: small errors keep growing, and "secular" terms such as \(t\sin t\) appear. If the nominal solution is exponentially stable, the approximation holds uniformly for all \(t \ge 0\), the same lesson as Chapter 9.

10.4 Averaging

Consider a system that changes slowly compared with a periodic forcing:

\[ \dot x = \varepsilon f(t, x, \varepsilon), \qquad f \text{ periodic in } t \text{ with period } T \]

Over one period \(x\) barely moves, so it mostly feels the average of the forcing. Replace \(f\) by its average:

\[ \dot{\bar x} = \varepsilon f_{av}(\bar x), \qquad f_{av}(x) = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T f(\tau, x, 0)\, d\tau \]
Averaging theorem (Theorem 10.4)

If the averaged system has an exponentially stable (hyperbolic) equilibrium \(p^*\), then for small \(\varepsilon\) the original system has a unique exponentially stable periodic solution of period \(T\) in an \(O(\varepsilon)\) neighborhood of \(p^*\), and \(x(t) - \bar x(t) = O(\varepsilon)\) for all \(t \ge 0\).

Try this

  • With \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) the two curves are almost the same; the actual one has a tiny ripple.
  • Increase \(\varepsilon\) toward 1. The ripple grows: the separation between slow and fast time scales is what makes averaging work.

10.5 Weakly nonlinear oscillators

For \(\ddot y + \omega^2 y = \varepsilon g(y, \dot y)\), write \(y = r\sin(\omega t + \phi)\). The amplitude \(r\) and phase \(\phi\) change slowly, and averaging their equations over one period gives an equation for the amplitude alone. Its stable equilibria are stable limit cycles. For the Van der Pol oscillator this gives \(\dot{\bar r} = \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\bar r(1 - \bar r^2/4)\), so the amplitude settles at \(r = 2\), the same answer as the describing function in Chapter 7, now with a proof for small \(\varepsilon\).

10.6 General averaging and vibrational control

Averaging does not need periodicity, only a well-defined average over long times. A classic application is vibrational stabilization: oscillating the pivot of an inverted pendulum vertically, fast enough, makes the upright position stable. The averaged system has an extra restoring term proportional to the square of the vibration's velocity amplitude, which can overcome gravity.

Key takeaways

A small parameter allows systematic approximation. Regular expansions are valid on finite intervals, and on infinite ones near exponentially stable solutions. Averaging replaces fast periodic forcing by its mean; a stable equilibrium of the averaged system corresponds to a stable periodic orbit of the real one.
